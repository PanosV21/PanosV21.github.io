Ownership Rights and the Direction of Innovation Evidence from the 1983 Patent Policy Change

Paper PDF
Abstract

This paper studies how the allocation of intellectual property rights over publicly funded inventions shapes the direction of private innovation and the diffusion of technological knowledge. I exploit the 1983 Presidential Memorandum on Government Patent Policy, which expanded contractors’ ability to retain ownership of future federally funded inventions while leaving the underlying research funding mechanism largely unchanged. Using a matched difference-in-differences design at the firm–technology level, I find that stronger contractor ownership reduced cross-technology spillovers by approximately 25–30 percent, lowered the share of technologically similar follow-on innovation occurring outside the originating firm, and increased private patent market value by 30–40 percent. The evidence indicates that stronger ownership rights changed the composition rather than the quantity of innovation, shifting research toward inventions with greater private appropriability but narrower external applicability. The findings identify ownership allocation as an important innovation policy instrument, showing that the allocation of rights over publicly funded research shapes not only firms’ incentives to innovate but also the social reach of the innovations they choose to pursue.

Financial Dependence and the Evolution of the U.S. Labor Share

Abstract

This paper examines the relationship between external financing and the U.S. labor share using data on 34 industries over 1970–2016. I find that industries more reliant on external finance exhibit lower labor-share growth, a relationship robust to controls for automation, offshoring, and import competition. The relationship reverses in 1970–1986, when financial dependence is positively associated with labor-share growth. To interpret these findings, I propose a mechanism through which relaxing financial constraints affects investment, technology adoption, and within-industry reallocation. The results document a changing relationship between financial dependence and labor shares, consistent with heterogeneous responses to financial liberalization, without establishing a causal effect of deregulation.

The Long Shadow of German Division Persistent Segmentation in Business Networks

Abstract

Does a political boundary continue to structure economic relationships after it disappears? I study networks among 292,273 German firms nearly three decades after reunification, identifying connections through individuals serving on both firms’ boards of directors. Combining geographic mapping with a spatial regression discontinuity approach around the former inner-German border and Berlin Wall, I compare connections between otherwise comparable nearby firms on the same and opposite sides. Cross-border connection probabilities are lower, with estimated gaps equivalent to 17–25 percent of the unconditional connection rate in broader inner-border samples and 25–45 percent in narrower samples. Segmentation is concentrated in northern and central border regions. In Berlin, the pattern is strongest near the former Wall in the city center, while pooled-city evidence is less robust to spatial clustering. These findings document persistent segmentation in formal business networks after political reunification, although the cross-sectional evidence limits causal and welfare conclusions.
